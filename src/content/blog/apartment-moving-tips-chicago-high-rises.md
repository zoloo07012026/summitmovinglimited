A high-rise move is rarely harder than a house move because of the furniture.
It is harder because of the building. Elevators have to be booked, docks have
to be free, and paperwork has to be filed before anyone can carry a box
through the lobby.

None of that is difficult once you know it is coming. It only causes problems
when it is discovered on move day.

## Ask the building three questions early

Before you book anything, call or email building management and ask:

- What are the move-in and move-out hours, and which days are allowed?
- Is the freight elevator reserved, and how far ahead?
- What paperwork does the building need from the moving company?

Many downtown and North Side buildings restrict moves to weekday business
hours, which rules out the Saturday everyone wants. Some require a refundable
deposit against damage to shared areas. A few limit moves to one household per
day, so the slot you want may already be taken.

The answers change what date you can book, so get them before you commit to
anything else.

## The certificate of insurance is the usual bottleneck

Most managed buildings will not let a crew start work without a certificate of
insurance on file. It is a one-page document from the mover's insurer, and the
building almost always wants specific things on it:

- The building's legal entity named as **additional insured**, spelled exactly
  as management writes it
- Specific coverage limits, often general liability and workers' compensation
- The correct move date

The delay is rarely the document itself. It is the back-and-forth when the
entity name is wrong or a limit is short. Ask management for their COI
requirements in writing, forward that to your mover as soon as your date is
set, and confirm the building has received and approved it a few days before
the move.

> If a building says the certificate is "on file" but cannot tell you the
> approval date, check again. An unapproved COI stops a crew in the lobby.

## Reserve the elevator, then plan around its size

A reserved freight elevator is a block of time, not a guarantee of speed. Two
things decide how the day actually goes.

**How long you have.** A three-hour window and a four-hour load are a problem
you want to find out about a week early, not at hour three. Tell your mover
the exact window when you book.

**What fits inside it.** Measure the elevator's interior depth and door height
if you own anything tall or rigid - a wardrobe, a sectional, a mattress that
does not bend, a treadmill. Something that will not fit means either
disassembly or the stairs, and both change how long the move takes.

## Sort out the truck before the truck arrives

Loading access is the second half of the problem:

- **Loading dock.** Ask for the height clearance. Some garages will not take a
  26-foot truck, which means a shuttle or a longer carry from the street.
- **Street parking.** Chicago requires a permit to reserve residential street
  space, and it has to be applied for in advance. Without one, the crew parks
  wherever they can and carries further.
- **Distance from the truck to the elevator.** A long carry is normal
  downtown. It is only a surprise if nobody mentioned it when the quote was
  written.

## What to have ready on the day

- Gate codes, dock codes and a phone number for the doorman or engineer
- Reserved parking marked off, if you have a permit
- Boxes labelled by room, with the new unit number on each one
- Anything fragile or high-value separated and pointed out during the
  walkthrough
- One box of essentials you carry yourself: chargers, medication, documents,
  a change of clothes

## Where high-rise moves usually go wrong

Three things account for most of the delays we hear about:

1. **The COI was sent late.** Everything else is ready and the crew waits.
2. **The elevator window was too short.** The building will not always extend
   it, and a second trip costs more than a longer booking would have.
3. **Nobody measured the tight thing.** A sofa that cannot turn the corner
   into the hallway becomes a disassembly job, on the clock.

All three are avoidable a week ahead and expensive on the day.
